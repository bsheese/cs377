## Part 1: Two Libraries, One Line

At the end of the last unit we arrived at the line of best fit. We had a scatterplot of shoe size and height, we had a way of measuring how wrong any given line was, and we had a recipe for finding the one line that was least wrong. The slope came from the correlation coefficient scaled by the ratio of the two standard deviations, and the intercept came from forcing the line through the point of means. We could, in principle, do all of that with a calculator and some patience.

In practice nobody does. Python has libraries that will fit the line for us, and the first thing worth saying about them is that there are two popular ones and students routinely assume they are competitors that give different answers. They are not. Scikit-learn and Statsmodels fit the same line to the same data and produce the same slope and the same intercept, down to as many decimal places as you care to print. They differ in what they are designed to help you do afterward.

Scikit-learn is built around prediction. Its entire vocabulary is "fit the model, then predict." You hand it your predictor and your outcome, it learns the relationship, and then it will take new values and hand you predicted values. It is comfortable, fast, and consistent: every model in the library works the same way, so once you have fit a line you already know how to fit a much more complicated model. What scikit-learn will not do is volunteer an opinion about whether the relationship you found is real. It will give you a number and let you decide what the number is worth.

Statsmodels is built around explanation. You fit the model and then ask it for a summary, and what comes back is a dense table: the coefficients, yes, but also standard errors, test statistics, p-values, confidence intervals, and several measures of overall fit. It is the tool you reach for when the question is not "what will the next value be?" but "is there actually a relationship here, and how sure can I be about its size?" It expects you to read the table, which means it expects you to know what the columns mean. Most of this unit is about learning to read that table by building each piece of it yourself.

Which one you use depends on the question you are asking. If you want to predict heights for students you have not met, scikit-learn is the natural fit. If you want to make a claim about the relationship between shoe size and height, and defend that claim, Statsmodels gives you the evidence.

Each library has one quirk that catches nearly everyone the first time. Scikit-learn insists that your predictors arrive as a two-dimensional table, even when you have only one predictor. A single column of shoe sizes looks one-dimensional to Python, and scikit-learn will refuse it until you reshape it into a table with one column. Statsmodels has the opposite kind of surprise: it will not add an intercept for you. If you forget to add one, it does not complain. It quietly fits a line that is forced to pass through the origin, which for our data would mean assuming that a student with a shoe size of zero has a height of zero. The model still runs. The answer is just wrong, and nothing in the output announces it. Statsmodels also expects the outcome first and the predictors second, which is the reverse of scikit-learn's order.

Both of those errors are worth committing to memory now, because both of them produce output rather than an error message, and output that looks reasonable is much harder to catch than a crash.

## Part 2: Did We Just Get Lucky?

Suppose we fit our line and the slope comes out positive: for every additional unit of shoe size, our model predicts some additional fraction of an inch of height. That is what we hoped for. But before we believe it, we should ask an uncomfortable question. Our slope was calculated from a particular sample of students. If shoe size and height had nothing to do with each other at all, would a sample like ours still have produced a slope this large just by chance?

This is the skeptic's question, and it deserves a real answer rather than a shrug. The version of the world the skeptic is proposing has a name: the **null hypothesis**. For a regression slope, the null hypothesis says the true slope is zero, meaning that in the population as a whole, knowing a student's shoe size tells you nothing about their height. Our job is to figure out how surprising our data would be if that were true.

The usual way this gets taught is with a formula, and there is a formula, and it works. But the formula tends to become something students apply rather than understand. So we are going to build the answer by simulation first, and then check the simulation against the formula. When the two agree, the formula stops being a magic incantation and becomes a shortcut for something you have already seen happen.

Start with the null world. We want data in which shoe size genuinely has no relationship to height, but which is otherwise exactly like our data. There is a clean way to manufacture this. Keep every shoe size exactly as measured and keep every height exactly as measured, but shuffle the heights so that each height is randomly reassigned to a different student's shoe size. Nothing about either variable's distribution changes. The tall students are still tall and the big feet are still big. The only thing destroyed is the pairing between them, which is precisely the thing the null hypothesis says does not exist anyway.

Now fit a line to the shuffled data and record the slope. It will not be exactly zero, because random pairings still produce a bit of accidental slope in one direction or the other. Do it again with a fresh shuffle and record that slope too. Do it a thousand times and you will have a thousand slopes, and their distribution shows you exactly what chance alone is capable of producing. This procedure is called a **permutation test**.

Then compare. Count how many of your shuffled slopes are at least as extreme as the slope you actually observed, and divide by the number of shuffles. That proportion is your **p-value**. If a quarter of the shuffled slopes are as large as yours, then chance produces results like yours routinely and you have learned very little. If none of them are, then your result is something chance is essentially unable to manufacture, and the null hypothesis looks like a poor description of the world.

Two things about p-values are worth being careful about, because both are commonly misstated. First, the p-value is the probability of seeing data this extreme *if the null hypothesis is true*. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Those sound similar and are not. Second, a simulated p-value can never be exactly zero. If you ran a thousand shuffles and none of them matched your result, the honest statement is that the p-value is less than one in a thousand, which is the floor your simulation can resolve. Running more shuffles lowers the floor but never reaches zero.

The p-value tells us whether there is a relationship. It does not tell us how precisely we have measured it, and that is a separate and equally important question. Our slope came from one sample of students. Had we walked into a different classroom and measured a different hundred students, we would have gotten a somewhat different slope. How different? That spread is called the **standard error** of the slope, and it is a statement about the precision of our estimate rather than about the spread of the data.

We cannot go back out and collect fifty more samples of a hundred students each. But we can do something that turns out to be a remarkably good substitute. Take our existing sample of a hundred students and draw a hundred students *from it*, with replacement, meaning that some students get drawn two or three times and others do not get drawn at all. The result is a new dataset of the same size, made of real observations, but not identical to the original. It is a plausible version of what a different sample from the same population might have looked like. Fit a line to it and record the slope. Repeat a couple of thousand times. The standard deviation of all those slopes is your standard error. This procedure is called the **bootstrap**.

It is worth pausing on the difference between the two simulations, because they look mechanically similar and students frequently blur them together. The permutation test shuffles the heights, deliberately breaking the connection between shoe size and height, in order to model a world where no relationship exists. The bootstrap resamples whole students, keeping each student's shoe size and height attached to each other, in order to model repeated sampling from a world where the relationship is whatever it actually is. Same machinery, opposite purposes. One destroys the relationship on purpose; the other protects it carefully.

With a standard error in hand, two more pieces of the summary table fall out easily. Dividing the slope by its standard error gives the **t-statistic**, which just expresses the slope in units of its own uncertainty. A slope of three when the standard error is one is a slope you can see clearly; a slope of three when the standard error is four is noise. That single ratio is what the formula-based p-value is computed from, and if you have done the permutation test you will find the two p-values agree closely.

The other piece is the **confidence interval**. Rather than a verdict, it gives a range of plausible values in the units of the actual problem, which is often much more useful. Take the estimate and go out roughly two standard errors in either direction and you have the conventional 95% interval. Alternatively, take your two thousand bootstrap slopes, sort them, and read off the values that cut off the bottom 2.5% and the top 2.5%. The two approaches agree, and once again the simulation is the thing that makes the formula legible.

The interpretation of a confidence interval is the single most commonly botched statement in applied statistics, so it is worth stating carefully. It is tempting to say that there is a 95% probability the true slope falls inside your interval. That is not what it means. The true slope is a fixed number; it is either inside your interval or it is not, and no probability attaches to that fact. The 95% describes the *procedure*. If you repeated the whole study many times and built an interval each time in exactly this way, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. What you cannot know is whether the particular interval sitting in front of you is one of the successes. The ones that miss look no different from the ones that hit.

Finally, a caution about sample size that will come back repeatedly. Everything above depends on the standard error, and the standard error shrinks as the sample grows. Collect more students and your interval narrows, your t-statistic grows, and your p-value drops, all without the underlying relationship changing at all. This means that with a large enough sample, an effect far too small to matter for any practical purpose will still come back statistically significant. Statistical significance answers the question "can we distinguish this from zero?" It does not answer the question "is this big enough to care about?" You have to answer the second one yourself, using knowledge of the subject rather than the output of the model.

> **Note:** The 0.05 threshold, below which results are conventionally called significant, is a convention and nothing more. It was proposed as a rough rule of thumb and hardened over decades into something treated as a law of nature. There is no meaningful difference between a p-value of 0.049 and one of 0.051, and treating the first as a discovery and the second as a failure has done real damage to the research literature. Report the number and let it be a number.

## Part 3: Checking the Assumptions

Everything in the previous section, from the standard error to the p-value to the confidence interval, rests on a set of assumptions about the data. The fitting itself does not: you can compute a line of best fit for any pile of numbers, and it will faithfully report the least-wrong straight line. But the inferential machinery, the part that says how sure we are, only produces trustworthy numbers when certain conditions hold. It is entirely possible to fit a model that reports an impressively small p-value and is nonetheless describing the data badly.

There are four conditions, and they are conventionally remembered with the acronym LINE: **Linearity**, **Independence**, **Normality**, and **Equal variance**. Each has a diagnostic, and most of the diagnostics are pictures rather than tests, which is deliberate. A picture shows you the shape of the problem; a test only tells you whether a threshold was crossed.

**Linearity** is the assumption that the relationship you are modeling with a straight line is in fact roughly straight. This sounds obvious and is easy to violate, because plenty of real relationships rise quickly and then level off, or accelerate, or bend. The diagnostic is to plot the residuals against the fitted values. Recall that a residual is what is left over after the line has done its work: the actual value minus what the line predicted. If the line has captured the trend properly, the leftovers should look like structureless scatter, a formless cloud centered on zero. If instead the residuals form a clear curve, arcing above the line at the extremes and below it in the middle, then the data is bending and your straight line is not following it. The residual plot is much better at revealing this than the original scatterplot, because it removes the overall trend and leaves only the mismatch, magnified.

**Independence** is the assumption that the observations do not carry information about each other. Our students, measured once each and unrelated to one another, are fine. The classic violation is data collected over time, where each observation tends to resemble the one before it. Daily temperatures are not independent; today's temperature tells you a great deal about tomorrow's. When observations are correlated in this way, you effectively have less information than your sample size suggests, and every standard error in the summary table is too small as a result. There is a numerical diagnostic for this, the Durbin-Watson statistic, which is built by comparing each residual to the one that preceded it. It lands near 2 when consecutive residuals are unrelated, drifts toward 0 when they resemble each other, and toward 4 when they alternate.

It is worth knowing that this statistic is only meaningful when the rows have a genuine order, usually time. If your dataset happens to be sorted by some variable for convenience, the Durbin-Watson statistic will happily report severe autocorrelation that is entirely an artifact of the sort order and has nothing to do with the data-generating process. Before treating a bad value as a problem, ask whether the row order means anything at all.

**Normality** is the assumption that the residuals follow a roughly bell-shaped distribution. This one causes more anxiety than it deserves. It is not required in order to fit the line, and with a reasonably large sample the inference is fairly robust to mild violations. It matters most when the sample is small or the residuals are severely skewed. The diagnostic is a Q-Q plot, which sorts your residuals and plots them against the values you would expect if they were perfectly normal. If they match, the points fall along a straight diagonal. Systematic departures show up as characteristic shapes: an S-curve indicates tails that are heavier or lighter than normal, and a bend at one end indicates skew. A Q-Q plot is more informative than a histogram here, because a histogram's appearance depends heavily on how you chose the bins, and the deviations that matter usually live in the tails, where a histogram has the fewest observations to show you.

**Equal variance**, sometimes called homoscedasticity, is the assumption that the scatter around the line is about the same everywhere. The diagnostic is the same residual plot you used for linearity, but now you are looking at the vertical spread rather than the center. The characteristic violation looks like a funnel: tightly clustered residuals at one end, widely spread residuals at the other. This pattern is extremely common in data involving money or size, where the amount of variation tends to grow with the magnitude of the thing being measured. Prediction errors for a small house are naturally measured in thousands of dollars; errors for a mansion are naturally measured in hundreds of thousands. When the spread grows like this, the model's reported uncertainty is a compromise between the two regions, which means it is too pessimistic where the data is tight and too optimistic where the data is loose.

Notice that two of the four conditions are checked with the same plot. Residuals against fitted values is the single most useful diagnostic in regression, and it is worth building the habit of looking at it every time, before looking at anything else in the summary table.

When an assumption fails, you have options, and none of them is "give up." Non-linearity and unequal variance can often be addressed by transforming a variable, which is the subject of Part 5. Violations of independence usually call for a model designed for the structure in question. And there are ways of computing standard errors that are robust to unequal variance, leaving the coefficients alone and only correcting the uncertainty around them. Before reaching for any of these, though, it is worth checking whether the problem is the model at all, or whether a handful of unusual observations are producing it.

## Part 4: When One Point Runs the Show

A single observation can, under the right circumstances, dominate a regression. Not influence it slightly, but genuinely determine its slope, to the point where removing that one row from a dataset of thousands visibly rotates the line. This is unsettling the first time you see it, and the defense against it is knowing what to look for.

The words we use here are precise and often confused, so it is worth separating them carefully.

**Leverage** is about the x-value alone. A point has high leverage when its predictor value is far from the average predictor value. The name is apt: think of the line as a beam balanced at the point of means. A point far out at the end of the beam can move it a great deal with very little force, while a point near the middle can push as hard as it likes and accomplish little. High leverage is potential, not action. A far-out point that happens to fall exactly where the trend predicts is well-behaved.

An **outlier**, in the regression sense, is about the y-value given the x-value. It is a point with a large residual, sitting far above or far below the line. A point can be an outlier without being unusual in either variable on its own: a student of perfectly ordinary height with perfectly ordinary feet can still be an outlier if the combination is unusual.

**Influence** is the thing we actually care about, and it is the product of the other two. A point is influential if removing it meaningfully changes the fitted coefficients. That generally requires both ingredients. A high-leverage point that follows the trend has the power to move the line but no reason to. An outlier sitting in the middle of the x-range has reason to move the line but very little power. A point that is both far out in x and far off the trend has both, and that is the combination that takes over a regression.

We would like a single number that captures this, and there is one: **Cook's distance**. The idea behind it is direct. For each observation, ask what would happen to every one of the model's predictions if that observation were removed and the model refit. Summarize that total displacement in a single value. A point with a large Cook's distance is one whose absence would change the model's output substantially across the board. Because it combines leverage and residual size into one measure, it catches the dangerous combination rather than either ingredient alone.

There is a common rule of thumb for what counts as large, based on the sample size, and it is worth using in the way rules of thumb should be used: as a way to decide which points to look at, not as a verdict. A flag is an instruction to investigate, not a sentence to be carried out.

This brings us to the part of the topic that is not mathematical. Having found an influential point, what should you do about it?

The tempting answer is to drop it. Resist that instinct, and be honest with yourself about the reason for it. There are legitimate reasons to remove an observation, and they all have the same character: you have determined that the data point is not a valid measurement of the thing you are studying. A data entry error, where a decimal point moved and a house sold for three million instead of three hundred thousand. A unit mismatch, where one row was recorded in centimeters and the rest in inches. An instrument failure. An observation that genuinely belongs to a different population than the one you are describing, such as a commercial property in a dataset of family homes. In each of these cases you are not removing an inconvenient point; you are correcting a data quality problem, and you should say so explicitly when you report the results.

There is exactly one illegitimate reason, and it wears many disguises: the point was hurting the fit. "It was lowering my R-squared" is not a justification. Neither is "the software flagged it." Cook's distance is a detective, not an executioner. It tells you where to look. It does not tell you what you will find, and it has no idea whether the observation is real.

If you investigate and conclude the point is genuine, you still have options. You can report both models, with and without the point, and let the reader see how much depends on it; this is honest and often the most informative thing you can do. You can use a form of regression that is designed to be less sensitive to extreme observations, which downweights points with large residuals rather than dropping them, giving you something between keeping and discarding. Or you can take the point seriously as information: a genuine observation that does not fit your model is telling you something about the limits of the model, and sometimes the right response is a better model rather than a smaller dataset.

## Part 5: Bending the Data Instead of the Line

Suppose you plot two variables and the relationship is clearly real but clearly not straight. A common shape looks like this: as the predictor increases from very low values, the outcome climbs steeply; as the predictor continues to increase, the climb flattens out. Income and life expectancy behave this way across countries. Going from very poor to modestly well-off buys an enormous amount of additional life expectancy, mostly through clean water, food security, and basic medicine. Going from well-off to very rich buys comparatively little.

Fit a straight line to that and everything goes wrong at once. The residual plot shows a pronounced arc, because the line cuts through a curve and must be too low in the middle and too high at both ends, or the reverse. The scatter is uneven as well. Two of the four LINE conditions fail together, and they fail for the same underlying reason.

The instinct at this point is to reach for a more complicated model. Often the better move is to keep the simple model and change the variable. This works because the requirement in linear regression is not that the relationship between your variables be a straight line. The requirement is that the model be linear in its parameters, meaning that it is built by adding up terms, each of which is a coefficient multiplied by something. What that something is remains up to you. If you replace the predictor with its logarithm, you are still fitting a straight line, still minimizing the same sum of squared residuals, still reading the same summary table. You have simply straightened the data before running it through the machinery.

The logarithm is the usual first choice for the shape described above, and there is a reason for it rather than a convention. The defining behavior of the log function is that it grows quickly at small values and slowly at large ones, and it does this in a specific way: each fixed *multiplication* of the input adds a fixed amount to the output. Doubling produces the same step whether you double from one thousand to two thousand or from a hundred thousand to two hundred thousand. That is exactly the structure of a diminishing-returns relationship, where what matters is proportional change rather than absolute change. Log is not a guess that happens to work; it is the transformation whose shape matches the mechanism.

Once you have transformed something, you have to be careful about interpretation, because the slope no longer means what it used to. If you have taken the log of the predictor, the slope is no longer "the change in y for a one-unit change in x," because a one-unit change in x means something different at different points on the scale. What the slope means instead is a change in y for a fixed *percentage* change in x. There is a convenient approximation here: divide the slope by one hundred and you have roughly the change in the outcome associated with a one percent increase in the predictor. That is often a far more natural statement about the world anyway. Saying that each additional dollar of national income buys some tiny fraction of a year of life expectancy is nearly meaningless; saying that each ten percent increase buys some sensible number of months is a claim a reader can actually evaluate.

You can transform the outcome instead of the predictor, or both, and each combination changes the interpretation. With the log on the outcome only, the slope becomes an approximate percentage change in the outcome for a one-unit change in the predictor. With logs on both, it becomes a percentage change for a percentage change, which economists call an elasticity. The arithmetic is the same in all three cases; only the sentence you write at the end changes.

Log is the most common transformation but not the only one. There are situations where the reciprocal is the natural choice, and often the subject matter tells you so before the data does. Fuel efficiency is a good example. Miles per gallon is a ratio with the thing you care about in the denominator, and the physics of moving a car relates fuel *consumed per mile* to engine size in a roughly linear way. Consumption per mile is the reciprocal of miles per gallon. So the transformation that straightens the relationship is not a numerical accident; it is what you would predict from knowing what the variables mean.

There are also procedures that will search for a good transformation automatically, working through a family of powers and reporting which one makes the residuals behave best. These are useful, and they are most useful as confirmation. When such a procedure lands on the log for an income variable, or on something close to the reciprocal for fuel efficiency, the value is that it agrees with the reasoning you had already done. When it produces something strange with no interpretation attached, that is a reason for suspicion rather than a discovery.

A few honest warnings are in order.

A transformation changes the question you are answering. A model of log income is a model of proportional differences, and if the question you were actually asked concerns absolute differences, you now have some translating to do. Related: if you fit a model on a transformed outcome and then convert the predictions back to the original scale, the conversion does not behave the way you would expect. Undoing a log on an average does not give you the average, and predictions converted back naively come out systematically too low. There are standard corrections for this, and the important thing at this stage is to know that the issue exists.

Interpretability declines as transformations accumulate. A model with a log on one side is easy enough to explain. A model with an unusual fractional power on both sides may fit beautifully and be nearly impossible to describe to anyone.

Finally, judge a transformation by the residual plot, not by R-squared. This is the discipline that matters most here. The point of the transformation was to fix a structural problem: a curve in the residuals, a funnel in the spread. The residual plot is where you can see whether the problem is fixed. R-squared, meanwhile, is not comparable across models with different outcome scales at all, so if you transformed the outcome, the two R-squared values you are tempted to compare are answering different questions and the comparison is meaningless.

## Part 6: Does It Work on Data It Has Not Seen?

There is a problem hiding in everything we have done so far, and it is easy to miss because it is a problem of procedure rather than arithmetic.

We fit our line to a sample of students. We then evaluated the line using the same sample of students. The line was chosen specifically to minimize its errors on those observations, so of course it does well on them. We have asked the model to sit an exam whose answer key it was allowed to study, and then treated the score as evidence of understanding. Whatever the fit statistic says, it is an optimistic number, and the amount of optimism it contains is unknown.

The fix is procedural and simple. Before fitting anything, set some of the data aside. Fit the model on the portion you kept, then evaluate it on the portion you set aside, which it has never seen. That held-out score is an honest estimate of how the model will perform on new observations, which is nearly always the thing you actually want to know.

The rule governing the held-out data is strict and worth stating plainly: it is off-limits for anything that shapes the model. Not for fitting, not for choosing which variables to include, not for deciding how to transform something, not for picking among several candidate models. Every time you look at the test data and make a decision based on what you see, you leak a little information from it into the model, and its score becomes a little less honest. Inspecting the held-out data for quality problems, such as impossible values or missing entries, is fine, because that is not a modeling decision.

Now, here is the result that surprises people. Do this with a simple model, a straight line with two parameters fit to a few thousand observations, and the training score and the held-out score come out essentially the same. Sometimes the held-out score is slightly higher, purely because that particular split happened to be a slightly easier sample. Split the data a different way and repeat, and the gap wanders around zero without going anywhere in particular.

This is worth sitting with, because the usual framing of this topic leads students to expect a dramatic gap. Simple models generalize well. A straight line has almost no capacity to memorize anything; it has two numbers with which to describe thousands of observations, and it cannot use them to accommodate individual quirks even if it wanted to. The optimism we were worried about is real but tiny, because there is very little for the model to be optimistic about.

The gap appears when the model has enough flexibility to chase the noise. The clearest way to see this is with a deliberately flexible model. Take data generated by a gentle wave with random noise added, and fit polynomials of increasing degree, meaning curves allowed to bend once, then twice, then many times. A straight line does poorly, because it cannot follow the wave at all: this is **underfitting**, and both the training score and the held-out score are bad together. A gentle curve, allowed a few bends, does well on both. Push the degree up to fifteen or twenty and something strange happens. The training score climbs toward perfection, because a sufficiently wiggly curve can pass very near every training point. The held-out score collapses. The curve has learned the particular noise in the training sample, and that noise is not present in the new data, so all the elaborate wiggling is worse than useless. This is **overfitting**.

One detail from that collapse is worth noticing, because it contradicts something students often half-remember. R-squared on held-out data can be negative. It is not a squared quantity that must sit between zero and one; it is a comparison against the baseline of always predicting the mean. Negative simply means the model performed worse on new data than a flat line at the average would have. An overfit model regularly manages this.

The pattern behind all of this has a standard framing. The error a model makes on new data can be split into three parts. Some of it is **bias**: error from the model being too rigid to represent the real pattern, which is what the straight line suffers from when the truth is a wave. Some of it is **variance**: error from the model being so flexible that it changes substantially depending on which sample it happened to be trained on. And some of it is irreducible, the noise in the data itself, which no model can predict and none can remove.

As you increase flexibility, bias falls and variance rises. Their sum falls at first, because early gains in flexibility buy real pattern-matching cheaply, and then rises, because later gains buy nothing but noise-chasing. The total traces a U, and the bottom of the U is the model you want. This is the **bias-variance tradeoff**, and it is one of the small number of ideas in this course that will keep reappearing regardless of what kind of model you eventually work with.

That is the arc of simple linear regression, from fitting one line through a cloud of points to asking whether that line will hold up on data it has never seen. Every idea here extends directly to models with more than one predictor, which is where we go next. The line becomes a plane and then something we can no longer draw, but the residuals, the assumptions, the influential points, the transformations, and the held-out test all work exactly the same way.
